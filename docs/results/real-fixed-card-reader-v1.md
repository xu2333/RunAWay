# An unchanged 575-character card produced two concrete cross-game plans with the revised reader

The owner asked how to learn an executable procedure from demonstrations. Two existing cards contained the same design goal and a proposed switch between discrete and continuous controls. In the [preceding experiment](real-control-switch-v1.md), both reads of the 575-character card applied the control switch but left transfer to another game unclear. Both reads of the 572-character card satisfied both criteria.

This run held each card and the current task byte-for-byte constant across its two readers. A generic system instruction asked the revised reader to identify which concrete design decision reflects each relevant goal at its stated scope. It contained no mention of games, transfer, controllers or the scoring criteria. No cards were generated or edited.

| Whole card | Reader | Usable plans | Concrete transfer across games | Distinct control methods | Both in the same usable plan |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 575 characters | Original | 2/2 | 0/2 (two partial) | 2/2 | 0/2 |
| 575 characters | Revised | 2/2 | 2/2 | 2/2 | 2/2 |
| 572 characters | Original | 2/2 | 1/2 (one unclear) | 2/2 | 1/2 |
| 572 characters | Revised | 2/2 | 2/2 | 2/2 | 2/2 |

## What changed in the plans

Both original-reader plans for the 575-character card mentioned eventual expansion to more games. They specified how menu and continuous actions could share a planning or verification layer inside the current task. The concrete work for a second game remained unspecified. A score based on merely mentioning more games would have missed this distinction.

One revised-reader plan for that card identified reusable demonstration recording, milestone representation, state assessment, memory and verification, then excluded direct reuse of one game's combat actions in the next. The other separated a shared demonstration store, task structure, memory and failure records from concrete control skills and per-demonstration task cards. Both described when a low-latency skill takes over. This locates the earlier omission in the reader's application of scope. The comparison does not identify which clause of the new instruction mattered or whether a length-matched generic reminder would have worked.

On the 572-character card, both revised plans named shared learning infrastructure and separately learned game-specific entries, states or skills. Its concurrent original reader achieved one joint pass, versus two in the earlier study. The new reader cleared both the prespecified recovery target on 575 and the two-pass guard on 572. These observations cover two fixed cards, one owner and one previously inspected question.

## Qualifications and attribution

All eight plans offered an executable design with verifiable completion and distinguished interface recognition from continuous control. Seven mentioned multi-touch, including all four revised reads. None turned the proposed controller into an owner mandate or a claim of validated performance. None explicitly identified the earlier assistant as its proposer. One revised 572 response described a milestone tap as indicating a successful segment; its later steps still required observable completion evidence. A tap records the owner's completion judgment and does not independently establish success. This was a wording qualification outside the primary endpoint. The revised deliveries focused on the learning design without reciting the card's unrelated visual preferences or device details.

The card contents and task were fixed. The measured difference concerns delivery, not a change in the encoded preferences. One local analyst knew the case and criteria, scored shuffled plans with assignments concealed and locked excerpts before revealing conditions. Prior observations were used only for context. No independent human acceptance, fresh-task generalization or device execution was measured.
